# Man and His Symbols — Reference

> Carl G. Jung (ed.), *Man and His Symbols* (1964). Conceived and edited by Jung and completed by his close collaborators after his death; the last work Jung wrote, and the only one aimed at a general audience. Page numbers below follow the standard one-volume edition's pagination.

A part-referenced index and summary of Jung's introduction to analytical psychology — the unconscious, dreams, archetypes, and symbols, told through five essays by Jung and four of his associates, richly illustrated. Use it to locate a part, recall its argument, and jump to the source pages.

---

## The book in one line

The unconscious speaks in **symbols** — above all through **dreams** — drawing on **archetypes**, the inherited patterns of the **collective unconscious**; reading these symbols is how a person pursues **individuation**, the lifelong becoming-whole of the self.

### What Jung means by a symbol (Part 1)

> "What we call a symbol is a term, a name, or even a picture that may be familiar in daily life, yet that possesses specific connotations in addition to its conventional and obvious meaning… A word or an image is symbolic when it implies something more than its obvious and immediate meaning. It has a wider 'unconscious' aspect that is never precisely defined or fully explained."

A **symbol** is distinct from a **sign**: a sign merely denotes a known thing; a symbol points beyond itself to something that cannot be fully grasped.

## Part summaries

### Introduction — John Freeman (p. 9)
Recounts how the book came to be: after the 1959 BBC *Face to Face* interview, Jung was persuaded to write — for the first time — a plain-language account of his ideas for the general reader. Jung agreed on the condition that his closest collaborators each contribute a chapter, and he supervised the whole plan. It became his final project, completed shortly before his death in 1961.

### Part 1 — Approaching the Unconscious — C. G. Jung (p. 18)
Jung's own essay and the conceptual core of the book. He explains how the unconscious communicates through symbols and why dreams are its chief vehicle.
Sections:
- **The importance of dreams** — symbols and the limits of conscious perception; the subliminal.
- **The past and the future in the unconscious** — dreams as both retrospective and anticipatory.
- **The function of dreams** — the **compensatory** role of dreams, balancing one-sided conscious attitudes.
- **The analysis of dreams** — why dreams must be read in the dreamer's context, not by fixed dream-dictionaries; against rigid Freudian free-association.
- **The problem of types** — the attitudes (introversion/extraversion) and four functions (thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition).
- **The archetype in dream symbolism** — archetypes as inherited, universal patterns of the **collective unconscious**.
- **The soul of man** — the modern loss of symbolic and instinctual life; alienation from nature.
- **The role of symbols** — symbols as the psyche's natural, healing language.
- **Healing the split** — reintegrating conscious and unconscious toward wholeness.

### Part 2 — Ancient Myths and Modern Man — Joseph L. Henderson (p. 104)
How archetypal patterns from myth and ritual persist in the dreams and lives of modern people.
Sections:
- **The eternal symbols** — recurring mythic motifs across cultures.
- **Heroes and hero makers / The archetype of initiation** — the hero myth as a model of ego development; rites of initiation, death-and-rebirth, the ordeal.
- **Beauty and the Beast** — the symbolism of taming the animal nature.
- **Orpheus and the Son of Man** — the redeemer figure; Dionysian vs. Orphic vs. Christian patterns.
- **Symbols of transcendence** — symbols (birds, journeys, the trickster) that free the psyche toward growth.

### Part 3 — The Process of Individuation — Marie-Louise von Franz (p. 158)
A systematic walk through Jung's central concept: the gradual integration of unconscious contents into a whole personality.
Sections:
- **The pattern of psychic growth** — individuation as a natural, organic process guided by the Self.
- **The first approach of the unconscious** — the initial confrontation with unconscious material.
- **The realization of the shadow** — recognizing and owning the rejected, dark side of the personality.
- **The anima: the woman within** — the unconscious feminine in a man.
- **The animus: the man within** — the unconscious masculine in a woman.
- **The Self: symbols of totality** — the Self as the regulating center and image of wholeness (mandala, the stone, the cosmic person).
- **The social aspect of the Self** — individuation's relation to community and to others.

### Part 4 — Symbolism in the Visual Arts — Aniela Jaffé (p. 230)
Archetypal symbolism traced through art, from prehistory to the modern era.
Sections:
- **Sacred symbols — the stone and the animal** — the stone and animal as the oldest carriers of the numinous.
- **The symbol of the circle** — the circle/mandala as an image of the Self and of cosmic order.
- **Modern painting as a symbol** — abstraction, the "secret soul of things," the **retreat from reality**, and the **union of opposites** in twentieth-century art.

### Part 5 — Symbols in an Individual Analysis — Jolande Jacobi (p. 272)
A single, sustained clinical case (the patient "Henry"), showing the theory in practice: how a series of dreams unfolds the individuation process across an analysis — including the famous **oracle dream** and encounters with the anima and Self imagery.

### Conclusion — Science and the Unconscious — Marie-Louise von Franz (p. 304)
Situates Jungian psychology alongside the natural sciences, arguing that the unconscious and its archetypes meet matter at a deep boundary (touching on synchronicity and the *unus mundus*), and defends the scientific seriousness of studying symbols.

## Key concepts

- **The Unconscious** — the part of the psyche outside awareness; for Jung it is creative and purposive, not merely a repository of repressed material.
- **Collective Unconscious** — a deep, inherited stratum of the psyche shared by all humans, the source of the archetypes.
- **Archetype** — an inherited, universal pattern or predisposition (the Hero, the Mother, the Shadow, the Wise Old Man) that shapes images and behavior; the form is universal, the content culturally filled.
- **Symbol vs. Sign** — a sign denotes a known thing; a **symbol** points to something that can never be fully defined — the natural language of the unconscious.
- **Dream / Compensation** — dreams express the unconscious and **compensate** for one-sided conscious attitudes, restoring psychic balance.
- **Individuation** — the central life-process of becoming a whole, distinct self by integrating conscious and unconscious contents.
- **Shadow** — the unrecognized, inferior, or rejected aspects of the personality, the first to be confronted in individuation.
- **Anima / Animus** — the contrasexual inner figure: the feminine in a man (anima), the masculine in a woman (animus); a bridge to the unconscious.
- **The Self** — the archetype of wholeness and the regulating center of the total psyche; often imaged as a **mandala**, circle, stone, or divine/cosmic person.
- **Psychological Types** — two attitudes (introversion / extraversion) and four functions (thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition).
- **Mandala** — a circular/symmetrical figure symbolizing the Self and the ordering of the psyche.

---

## Notes on the text

- This is a **multi-author** volume planned and edited by Jung; only Part 1 is by Jung himself. The other parts are by Henderson, von Franz, Jaffé, and Jacobi, each writing under Jung's overall scheme.
- It was written for a **general audience** and is heavily illustrated — the images are integral to the argument, not decoration.
- It is Jung's **last work**, finished within days of his death in June 1961.
