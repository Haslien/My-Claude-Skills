# The Poetics of Aristotle — Reference

> Aristotle, *The Poetics*, in S. H. Butcher, *Aristotle's Theory of Poetry and Fine Art, with a Critical Text and Translation of the Poetics* (4th ed., Macmillan). Citations use the standard **chapter number (I–XXVI)** and **Bekker reference** (e.g. 1449b24), which are stable across all editions; the Butcher edition prints Bekker numbers in the margins.

A chapter-referenced index and summary of Aristotle's *Poetics* — the founding work of Western literary theory and dramatic criticism. The treatise as we have it is incomplete (the discussion of comedy is lost) and survives as compressed lecture notes. Use this to locate a chapter, recall its argument, and cite it.

## The Poetics in one line

Poetry is **mimesis** (imitation/representation), and the highest poetic form is **tragedy**, whose soul is **plot** — a unified action that, through pity and fear, achieves **catharsis**.

### The definition of tragedy (Ch. VI, 1449b24)

> "Tragedy, then, is an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude; in language embellished with each kind of artistic ornament, the several kinds being found in separate parts of the play; in the form of action, not of narrative; through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation [*catharsis*] of these emotions."

## Structural map (chapters & Bekker references)

The treatise has 26 chapters. Butcher groups the argument as: foundations of imitation (I–V), the definition and parts of tragedy (VI), the theory of plot (VII–XIV, XVI), character (XV), practical rules for the poet (XVII–XVIII), thought and diction (XIX–XXII), epic poetry (XXIII–XXIV), critical objections (XXV), and the comparison of epic and tragedy (XXVI).

## Key concepts

- **Mimesis (μίμησις)** — Imitation/representation; the common ground of all the arts. Distinguished by *medium*, *objects*, and *manner* (Ch. I–III).
- **Mythos (μῦθος) / Plot** — The arrangement of the incidents; "the first principle and, as it were, the soul of tragedy" (Ch. VI). Must be a unified whole of adequate magnitude (Ch. VII–VIII).
- **Catharsis (κάθαρσις)** — The "purgation" / clarification of pity and fear that tragedy effects (Ch. VI). The single most debated term in the work.
- **Hamartia (ἁμαρτία)** — The error, frailty, or miscalculation that brings the otherwise good hero from fortune to misfortune (Ch. XIII).
- **Peripeteia (περιπέτεια) / Reversal** — A change of the action to its opposite (Ch. XI).
- **Anagnorisis (ἀναγνώρισις) / Recognition** — A change from ignorance to knowledge; most powerful when coinciding with reversal (Ch. XI, XVI).
- **Pathos (πάθος)** — A destructive or painful action — death, agony, wounding — as a plot component (Ch. XI).
- **Desis & Lusis** — Complication and Dénouement: the tying and untying of the plot (Ch. XVIII).
- **The Universal vs. the Particular** — Poetry tells what *may* happen (the universal, by probability or necessity); history tells what *did* happen. This makes poetry "more philosophical" than history (Ch. IX).
- **The Six Elements of Tragedy** — Plot, Character, Thought, Diction, Song (Melody), Spectacle — ranked in that order of importance (Ch. VI).
- **Probability & Necessity** — The governing law of plot and character: events should follow one another by probable or necessary sequence (Ch. VII–IX, XV).

## Notes on the text

- The *Poetics* is incomplete: Aristotle promises a treatment of **comedy** (Ch. VI), but the surviving book covers only tragedy and epic. A lost second book is presumed to have dealt with comedy.
- Several chapters (XII, XX, and part of XXI) are widely regarded as later **interpolations**, as Butcher's analysis notes.
- The work reads as terse lecture notes rather than a polished treatise, which accounts for its compression and occasional disorder.
