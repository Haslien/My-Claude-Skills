# The Hero with a Thousand Faces — Reference

> Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, Commemorative Edition (Princeton/Bollingen). Page numbers below follow that edition's printed pagination.

A page-referenced index and summary of Campbell's study of the **monomyth** — the single archetypal story Campbell argues underlies the world's myths, religions, and folk tales. Use it to locate a stage, recall its function, and jump to the source pages.

---

## The monomyth in one line

> "A hero ventures forth from the world of common day into a region of supernatural wonder: fabulous forces are there encountered and a decisive victory is won: the hero comes back from this mysterious adventure with the power to bestow boons on his fellow man." — p. 23

The **nuclear unit** of the monomyth is the rite-of-passage formula: **separation → initiation → return** (p. 23).

## Stage summaries

### Prologue — The Monomyth (p. 1)
Campbell sets up his method: myths are read as collective dreams, decoded with the "grammar of symbols" (he leans on psychoanalysis as a key, not a final word). He establishes the hero as a figure of exceptional gifts who, with the world suffering a "symbolical deficiency," undertakes the universal adventure. The **World Navel** (p. 37) is the symbolic source point — the axis from which creative power flows into the world.

### Part One — The Adventure of the Hero

#### Departure (Chapter I, p. 45)
- **The Call to Adventure (45)** — A herald or sign disrupts ordinary life and summons the hero toward the unknown; "the signs of the vocation of the hero."
- **Refusal of the Call (54)** — Fear or obligation makes the hero decline; "the folly of the flight from the god." The refused call can turn the adventure into its negative.
- **Supernatural Aid (63)** — The committed hero meets a protective figure (often the wise old man/woman) who supplies amulets and advice against the coming forces.
- **The Crossing of the First Threshold (71)** — The hero passes the "threshold guardian" at the edge of the known world into the zone of magnified power.
- **The Belly of the Whale (83)** — Crossing the threshold reads as a death: the hero is swallowed into the unknown, a passage into the realm of night and rebirth.

#### Initiation (Chapter II, p. 89)
- **The Road of Trials (89)** — A succession of tests and ordeals; "the dangerous aspect of the gods." The hero is repeatedly tested and aided.
- **The Meeting with the Goddess (100)** — Mystical union with the Magna Mater / Queen Goddess of the World; "the bliss of infancy regained."
- **Woman as the Temptress (111)** — The temptation to fall away from the spiritual quest into the lures of the flesh; "the realization and agony of Oedipus."
- **Atonement with the Father (116)** — Reconciliation with the ultimate power/authority figure who holds both terror and grace; the hero confronts and is reconciled with the father-creator.
- **Apotheosis (138)** — The hero transcends duality and is divinized — a state of expanded, godlike consciousness and rest in understanding.
- **The Ultimate Boon (159)** — Attainment of the goal: the life-giving elixir, knowledge, or power the quest was for.

#### Return (Chapter III, p. 179)
- **Refusal of the Return (179)** — Having found bliss, the hero may refuse to come back; "the world denied."
- **The Magic Flight (182)** — If the boon was taken against the guardians' will, the return becomes a pursuit and obstacle-flight; "the escape of Prometheus."
- **Rescue from Without (192)** — The world must come and assist a hero reluctant or unable to return.
- **The Crossing of the Return Threshold (201)** — Re-entry into the world of common day, carrying and validating the transcendent wisdom there.
- **Master of the Two Worlds (212)** — Freedom to pass back and forth between the inner/spiritual and outer/material worlds without contaminating one with the other.
- **Freedom to Live (221)** — Release from the fear of death; living in the present moment as the boon's fruit; "the nature and function of the ultimate boon."

#### The Keys (Chapter IV, p. 227)
Campbell reviews the full pattern, noting that any given tale may omit elements, contract, or stress others — yet the morphology of adventure, the character roles, and the victories vary astonishingly little. The boon's scope distinguishes hero types: the **fairy-tale hero** wins a domestic, microcosmic triumph; the **myth hero** wins a world-historical, macrocosmic one; **tribal/local heroes** (Moses, Huang Ti) serve one folk, while **universal heroes** (Buddha, Jesus, Mohammed) bring a message for all.

### Part Two — The Cosmogonic Cycle (p. 237)
The wider mythological vision that the successful hero's adventure opens onto: the creation, maintenance, and dissolution of the universe.
- **Emanations (237)** — The forms of the universe come out of the void; the One breaks into the manifold.
- **The Virgin Birth (275)** — The creative and redemptive role of the female power, on a cosmic scale (Mother of the Universe) and a human one (Mother of the Hero).
- **Transformations of the Hero (291)** — The legendary history of humankind through the hero's recurring forms — warrior, lover, emperor/tyrant, world-redeemer, saint — each appearing as the age requires.
- **Dissolutions (337)** — The foretold end, first of the hero, then of the manifested world (microcosm and macrocosm).

### Epilogue — Myth and Society (p. 351)
Myth's social functions and the predicament of the modern individual. **The Shapeshifter (353)** and **The Function of Myth, Cult, and Meditation (354)** frame myth as a living instrument; **The Hero Today (358)** argues that, with the old mythologies fragmented, the modern hero's quest turns inward — the recovery of the lost dimensions of the psyche becomes the contemporary adventure.

## Key terms

- **Monomyth** — Campbell's term (borrowed from Joyce's *Finnegans Wake*) for the single underlying pattern of the hero adventure: separation → initiation → return.
- **Hero with a thousand faces** — The one archetypal hero who appears in countless cultural masks across myth and religion.
- **World Navel (axis mundi)** — The symbolic center point from which creative energy flows into the world (p. 37).
- **Threshold / Threshold Guardian** — The boundary of the known world and the figure who guards passage into the unknown.
- **Magna Mater / Goddess** — The world-mother whose meeting represents totality and the bliss of reunion.
- **Boon / Elixir** — The life-renewing prize the hero brings back for the community.
- **Master of the Two Worlds** — The realized capacity to move freely between inner (spiritual) and outer (material) realms.
- **Cosmogonic Cycle** — The macro-myth of the universe's emanation, maintenance, and dissolution (Part Two).
- **Symbolical deficiency** — The lack or wound in the hero or the world that sets the adventure in motion.
